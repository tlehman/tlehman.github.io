From the Circle to Black Holes: a Mathematical Roadmap

I just finished writing two articles, Introduction to Fiber Bundles and Introduction to Differential Forms, and I want to step back and look at where they point. The articles spent all their time on the circle, and that was deliberate: the circle is small enough to render in a browser and rich enough to contain the two ideas that run modern physics. First, that spaces can be locally boring and globally twisted. Second, that calculus can be rebuilt so it detects the twist.

Here is the thing I find motivating enough to organize years of learning around: those two ideas, pushed hard enough, produce pictures of black holes and the equations of the Standard Model. Not metaphorically. The light-bending around a black hole is a statement about geodesics of a metric, and a force field is a statement about the curvature of a bundle. Both are things a rendering engine can compute, pixel by pixel, if you build the mathematics into it.

So this page is a roadmap with two summits: a general-relativistic renderer that draws what a black hole actually looks like, and a working understanding of gauge theory. Every stage produces an interactive page or a piece of software, because the rule I've adopted from the first two articles is: no idea gets to stay abstract. If I can't put a slider on it, I don't understand it yet.

What we have so far

Stage 0: bundles and forms ✓

Manifolds, charts, the tangent bundle, triviality and the Möbius counterexample, structure groups. Then the exterior calculus: forms, \( d \), \( d^2 = 0 \), closed versus exact, and the generalized Stokes theorem \( \int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega \), with the angle form \( d\theta \) as the recurring character that ties calculus to topology.

Artifacts: bundles, forms.

The road to black holes

Everything in the two articles was calculus without geometry: nothing could measure a length or an angle. The road to general relativity is the road of adding geometric structure, one instrument at a time.

Stage 1: connections and parallel transport

The tangent spaces of a manifold are separate rooms, and a connection is the rule for carrying a vector from room to room. The punchline is holonomy: carry a vector around a closed loop on a sphere and it comes back rotated. That rotation is the Möbius flip again, now with a continuous group of possible twists instead of \( \mathbb{Z}/2 \).

Artifact: an interactive sphere where you draw a loop and watch a tangent vector get parallel transported around it, with the holonomy angle displayed. Three.js, same style as the Möbius demo.

Stage 2: metrics, curvature, geodesics

A Riemannian metric is a smoothly varying inner product on the fibers of the tangent bundle, and it buys everything at once: lengths, angles, a preferred connection (Levi-Civita), curvature, and geodesics, the straightest possible paths. This is where Gauss's Theorema Egregium from the first article gets its modern form: curvature is the holonomy of the metric's connection, per unit area.

Artifact: a geodesic tracer on surfaces of revolution. Click a point and a direction, watch the geodesic wrap the surface. The geodesic equations are a small ODE system, which is the same computational core the black hole renderer will need.

Stage 3: special relativity and the Minkowski metric

Swap the inner product's signature to \( (-,+,+,+) \) and geometry becomes causality: vectors are timelike, spacelike, or null, and light rays are null geodesics. Nothing else in the toolkit changes, which is the great surprise of the subject.

Artifact: an interactive spacetime diagram with boosts as hyperbolic rotations, sliders for velocity, and the invariant interval computed live.

Stage 4: general relativity and the black hole renderer

Einstein's field equations \[ R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2} R\, g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\, T_{\mu\nu} \] say that the metric is not chosen, it is solved for. The first exact solution, Schwarzschild's, \[ ds^2 = -\left(1 - \tfrac{2GM}{r}\right) dt^2 + \left(1 - \tfrac{2GM}{r}\right)^{-1} dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2 \] is a black hole. Rendering one is conceptually simple and that's the beauty of it: a ray tracer where the rays obey the geodesic equation. For each pixel, integrate a null geodesic backwards from the camera through curved spacetime, and see whether it hits the accretion disk, escapes to the star field, or falls through the horizon. Gravitational lensing, the photon sphere at \( r = 3GM \), and the Einstein ring all appear automatically, because they were never special effects, they were geodesics all along.

Artifacts, in order: a 2D lensing demo in canvas (geodesics in the equatorial plane), then a full WebGL fragment-shader renderer where each pixel integrates the geodesic ODE on the GPU, then the Kerr (spinning) case with frame dragging.

The road to gauge theory

The second summit reuses the same instruments, aimed at bundles other than the tangent bundle. This is the road the articles were secretly walking the whole time: U(1) appeared as our first Lie group, and \( d\theta \) as our first connection-like object.

Stage 5: principal bundles and connections as fields

A principal \( G \)-bundle is a bundle whose fiber is the group \( G \) itself, our U(1) being the first interesting case. A connection on it is a Lie-algebra valued 1-form \( A \), and its curvature is \[ F = dA + A \wedge A \] For U(1) the wedge term dies and \( F = dA \) is exactly the electromagnetic field from the forms article. For \( SU(2) \) and \( SU(3) \) the wedge term survives, the field interacts with itself, and that self-interaction is why the strong force is hard and quarks are confined. The Aharonov–Bohm effect from the forms article is the experimental proof that the connection, not just the field, is physically real: holonomy you can measure with an interferometer.

Artifact: an Aharonov–Bohm interference simulator: a solenoid, two electron paths, a slider for the enclosed flux, and the interference pattern shifting as the holonomy \( \oint A \) changes.

Stage 6: Yang–Mills and the lattice

The Yang–Mills action \( S = \int \mathrm{tr}(F \wedge {\star}F) \) makes curvature itself the thing nature economizes. The computational approach that actually works is the lattice: put the gauge group on the edges of a grid, holonomy around each little square approximates \( F \), and you can simulate the theory with nothing but matrix multiplication. A lattice U(1) or SU(2) toy in the browser is entirely feasible and would make "curvature of a bundle" as concrete as the Möbius slider.

Artifact: a 2D lattice gauge simulator with plaquette holonomies visualized, temperature slider, and Wilson loops measured live.

Why one roadmap and not two

Look at what the two summits have in common. The black hole renderer integrates geodesics of a connection on the tangent bundle. The gauge simulator integrates holonomies of a connection on a principal bundle. Both are, at the level of code, the same loop: solve an ODE defined by a connection, accumulate the twist. That's the payoff of learning physics through bundles: general relativity and the Standard Model stop being two subjects and become two instances of one pattern, differing in which bundle and which group. Einstein unified gravity with geometry, Yang and Mills unified the rest of the forces with geometry, and the geometry is in both cases the geometry of a fiber bundle.

The circle was never a toy. It was the smallest laboratory where every one of these instruments could be built and tested. Now we get to point them at bigger game.
